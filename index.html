Clarity turns a city's cameras into one coordinated search. When someone goes missing, the alert doesn't broadcast. It travels the camera network itself, expanding along real travel times, handing context from one camera to the next until someone is found.

The Problem
The first hour is everything
01

Alerts go out, not outward

An alert is broadcast to everyone at once, or to a radius on a map. But nobody moves in a circle. People move along streets, transit lines and sidewalks, and a radius searches mostly places they could never have reached.

02

Cameras never talk to each other

Every feed is watched alone. The camera two blocks north has no idea what the camera behind the station just saw, so the same person is found from scratch, over and over, by whoever happens to be looking.

03

The search area grows as time squared

Someone on foot is inside 0.8 square miles after ten minutes and 7 square miles after thirty. Every minute spent organising the search costs more than the last one did.

Describe them.
We search.

Where they were last seen, when, what they were wearing, and how they travel. No photo, no ID, no biometrics. That is the whole interface, and it is everything the network needs.

Step one.
The graph.

Every camera is a node. Every edge is reachability, not proximity: an edge from one camera to the next carries how long it actually takes to travel between them on foot, by car, by transit. The map is the algorithm.

Step two.
The cone.

The alert is a walker. It spawns at the point last seen and expands outward only along edges it could have been travelled in the time elapsed. A camera nine minutes away isn't watching yet. It starts at t+9.

Step three.
The handoff.

Arriving at a camera, the walker hands its vision agent context, not a query: red jacket, blue duffel, last seen six minutes ago heading north, moderate confidence. Each camera starts where the last one left off.

# the alert is not a message. it is a walker. walker Bolo { has description: str; has origin_ts: int; has trail: list; can hunt with Camera entry { hit = watch(here.feed, self.description, self.trail); if hit.matched { self.trail.append(here); self.origin_ts = hit.ts; # re-bloom } visit [-->](?travel_s <= elapsed(self.origin_ts)); } }

Step four.
Re-bloom.

On a hit the walker relocates to that camera, resets its clock and blooms again from the new position. The confirmed path is written back into the graph as edges. On a miss across the whole frontier, confidence decays, the cone widens, and eventually the search retires itself.

Why a cone beats a circle
A radius searches water

A circle drawn around the point last seen grows as the square of elapsed time. At walking pace it is under a square mile after ten minutes and roughly twenty-eight after an hour.

Most of that circle is unreachable. It is water, freeway, fenced lot, the inside of a block. A radius spends the first hour searching places nobody could have walked to.

The cone only expands along edges that can actually be travelled in the time available, so the same hour buys a search that is far smaller and far more likely to contain them.

Built to be questioned
No faces. Ever.
01
Descriptions, not identities
The network only ever knows "red jacket, blue duffel". No face embeddings, no enrolment database, nothing that can be turned into a watchlist later. It cannot recognise a person it was not told to look for.
02
Searches expire on their own
Confidence decays with every frontier that comes back empty. A search that stops finding anything retires itself rather than running quietly forever.
03
Every search leaves a trail
The walker writes its own path into the graph, so there is a permanent record of exactly which cameras were activated, when, and why. Auditable by construction, not by policy.
Why this, why now
A child wanders off at a transit station and the cameras that could find her are already there, watching nothing in particular. Clarity is the hour that connects them.
